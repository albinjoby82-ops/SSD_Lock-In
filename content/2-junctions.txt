@@topic 8 | Bringing two materials into contact | Tutorial Ch2 Q2, Q3, Q4
@notes
## The one rule

> With no voltage applied, **$E_F$ is one flat line** through everything that is touching.

All of Chapter 2 is built on this.

## Why: think of water

$E_F$ is like a water level. It tells you how full of electrons a material is.

Take two tanks with different water levels and join them with a pipe.
Water runs from the high one to the low one. It stops when the levels are the same.

Electrons do exactly that. They run from the material with the **higher** $E_F$ to the one with the **lower** $E_F$, until the two levels match.

## Before contact

Draw both materials under one shared vacuum level (Topic 6).

{{fig:contact_before}}

Both are silicon, so $\chi$ and $E_G$ are the same. $E_C$ and $E_V$ line up.

Only $E_F$ is different:

- n-Si: $E_F$ near $E_C$. High. Small $\phi$.
- p-Si: $E_F$ near $E_V$. Low. Big $\phi$.

## What happens when they touch

1. Electrons flow from the n-Si (high $E_F$) into the p-Si (low $E_F$).
2. The n-side has lost electrons, so it is left a bit **positive**.
3. The p-side has gained electrons, so it becomes a bit **negative**.
4. A negative region is a high-energy place for an electron. So all the bands on the **p-side move up**. On the n-side they move down.
5. This carries on until the two $E_F$ lines are level. Then the flow stops.

? Which way do electrons flow when the materials touch: from high $E_F$ to low, or low to high?

= From high $E_F$ to low $E_F$. Like water running downhill.

## After contact

{{fig:contact_after}}

Far from the join, each side looks just like it did before: $E_C$ and $E_V$ are the same distance from $E_F$ as they always were.

But $E_F$ is now level. So the two sets of bands sit at **different heights**, and they bend smoothly to join up.

## How to draw it, every time

1. Draw $E_F$ as one flat line right across.
2. On the n-side, far from the join: put $E_C$ just **above** $E_F$, and $E_V$ a gap below that.
3. On the p-side, far from the join: put $E_V$ just **below** $E_F$, and $E_C$ a gap above that.
4. Join $E_C$ to $E_C$ and $E_V$ to $E_V$ with a smooth S-shaped curve.

The size of the step is the difference between the two workfunctions:

$$\text{step} = \phi_p - \phi_n$$

? n-Si with $\phi_n = 4.17$ eV touches p-Si with $\phi_p = 5.0$ eV. How big is the step in the bands?

= $5.0 - 4.17 = 0.83$ eV.

@@topic 9 | Metal on silicon: ohmic and Schottky contacts | Tutorial Ch2 Q6
@notes
## What a metal looks like on a band diagram

A metal has no gap. It is just full of electrons up to $E_F$.

So draw a metal as **one line**, its $E_F$, with a workfunction $\phi_m$ down from the vacuum level.

## Same rule as Topic 8

Electrons flow from the higher $E_F$ to the lower $E_F$ until it is flat.

Bigger workfunction = lower $E_F$.

So if $\phi_m > \phi_{Si}$, the metal's $E_F$ is lower, and **electrons leave the silicon and go into the metal**.

A metal has so many electrons that nothing changes inside it. All the bending happens **in the silicon**.

Silicon that has lost electrons near the join has its bands bent **up** towards the metal.

## Is losing electrons good or bad? It depends on the type

**p-Si.** Losing electrons is the same as gaining holes.
Holes are the majority carrier. So now there are **more** carriers at the join.
Nothing is in the way. Current flows easily both ways.
This is an **ohmic** contact. Its I–V curve is a straight line, like a resistor.

**n-Si.** Electrons are the majority carrier. Losing them leaves the join **empty** of carriers.
The bent-up bands are a **hill** that electrons must climb to reach the metal.
Current only flows one way.
This is a **Schottky** contact. Its I–V curve looks like a diode.

{{fig:ms}}

> **One line to remember.**
>
> **Ohmic** = more majority carriers at the join than in the rest of the Si.
>
> **Schottky** = fewer majority carriers at the join.

## All four cases

| Si type | Workfunctions | Electrons flow | Majority carriers at the join | Contact |
| p | $\phi_m > \phi_{Si}$ | Si → metal | more holes | **ohmic** |
| n | $\phi_m > \phi_{Si}$ | Si → metal | fewer electrons | **Schottky** |
| n | $\phi_m < \phi_{Si}$ | metal → Si | more electrons | **ohmic** |
| p | $\phi_m < \phi_{Si}$ | metal → Si | fewer holes | **Schottky** |

You do not need to memorise the table. Work it out each time:

1. Which way do electrons flow? (From the smaller $\phi$ to the bigger $\phi$.)
2. Does the Si end up with more or fewer of its majority carrier?

? A metal with $\phi_m$ smaller than $\phi_{Si}$ is put on n-Si. Ohmic or Schottky?

= Electrons flow from the metal into the Si. The n-Si gets more electrons, its majority carrier. So it is ohmic.

## Adding a voltage

A voltage adds its own field to the one already at the join.

**Ohmic.** Whichever way round the voltage is, the carriers piled at the join can move. Current flows **both ways**.

**Schottky (n-Si).**

- + on the metal: the hill gets lower. Electrons pour over it. **Current flows.**
- − on the metal: the hill gets higher. **No current**, just a tiny leak.

Under a voltage, $E_F$ is no longer flat. The two sides are separated by $eV$.

@@topic 10 | The pn junction with no voltage | Tutorial Ch2 Q5, Q11a
@notes
## Building a pn junction, step by step

Put p-Si against n-Si. This is the same as Topic 8, but now look at what the carriers do.

1. The p-side is full of holes. The n-side has almost none. So holes **diffuse** across into the n-side. (Topic 4: crowded to empty.)
2. Same for electrons, the other way: they diffuse from n into p.
3. Where they meet, electrons fall into holes and both vanish. So near the join there are **no carriers left**.
4. That empty strip is the **depletion region**.
5. What is left in it? The dopant atoms. They cannot move. On the p-side they are **negative**. On the n-side they are **positive**.
6. Positive on one side, negative on the other: that is an **electric field**, pointing from n to p.
7. This field pushes holes back to the p-side and electrons back to the n-side. It **stops** the diffusion.

{{fig:pn_ions}}

It settles when the push from the field exactly cancels the diffusion. Then no current flows.

? In the depletion region, what is the charge on the n-side: positive or negative? Why?

= Positive. The electrons have left. What remains is donor atoms, and each one gave away an electron, so it is positive.

## The same thing as a band diagram

{{fig:pn_band0}}

Use the Topic 8 recipe: $E_F$ flat, p-side bands high, n-side bands low, smooth bend between.

The step is a **barrier**. An electron on the n-side has to climb it to get to the p-side. A hole on the p-side has to get "down" it, which for a hole is also a climb.

The height of the step is $eV_0$. $V_0$ is the **built-in voltage**.

## The built-in voltage

$$V_0 = \frac{kT}{e}\,\ln\!\left(\frac{N_A\,N_D}{n_i^{2}}\right)$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Value |
| $V_0$ | built-in voltage. The formula sheet calls it $V_{bi}$. | V |
| $kT/e$ | thermal voltage | 0.026 V |
| $N_A$ | doping of the p-side | cm⁻³ |
| $N_D$ | doping of the n-side | cm⁻³ |
| $n_i^2$ | | $2.1\times10^{20}$ cm⁻⁶ |

> **In words.** More doping on either side → bigger barrier. It is usually between 0.6 and 0.9 V.
>
> **Use it when** you are asked for the built-in voltage, the barrier height, or the "contact potential".

**Example.** $N_A = N_D = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³.

$$V_0 = 0.026\,\ln\!\frac{10^{16}\times10^{16}}{2.1\times10^{20}} = 0.026\,\ln(4.76\times10^{11}) = 0.026\times26.9 = 0.70\ \text{V}$$

? You make $N_A$ ten times bigger. Does $V_0$ go up or down?

= Up. A bigger number inside the log. It rises by $0.026\,\ln(10) = 0.06$ V.

## Another way to see V_0

On the n-side, $E_F$ is above $E_i$ by some amount. On the p-side, $E_F$ is below $E_i$ by some amount.

$E_F$ is flat, so $E_i$ must step by the **sum** of those two. That sum is $eV_0$.

Tutorial Q5 uses this.

@@topic 11 | The pn junction with a voltage | Tutorial Ch2 Q7, Q8
@notes
## Where we start

With no voltage there is a barrier of height $eV_0$ (Topic 10).

It stops the majority carriers from diffusing across.

A voltage changes the **height of that barrier**. That is the whole story of the diode.

{{fig:pn_bias}}

## Forward bias: + on the p-side

The applied voltage $V$ pushes **against** the built-in voltage.

$$\text{barrier} = e\,(V_0 - V)$$

The hill is lower. Now lots of carriers have enough energy to get over:

- **holes** go from p to n
- **electrons** go from n to p

They cross by **diffusion**, from where there are many to where there are few.

There are huge numbers of majority carriers waiting, so the current is **large**. And it grows very fast (exponentially) with $V$.

The depletion region gets **thinner**.

## Reverse bias: + on the n-side

Now the applied voltage **adds** to the built-in voltage.

$$\text{barrier} = e\,(V_0 + |V|)$$

The hill is higher. No majority carrier can climb it. Diffusion across the junction stops.

So what current is left? Only this:

- On the p-side there are a **few electrons** (minority carriers). If one wanders to the edge of the depletion region, the field sweeps it down the hill to the n-side.
- Same for the **few holes** on the n-side, swept to the p-side.

That is a **drift** current of **minority** carriers.

There are very few of them: only $n_i^2/N$ per cm³. So the current is **tiny**.

And making the voltage bigger does not help. The field is already taking every minority carrier that arrives. The current is limited by **how many there are**, not by how hard you pull.

The depletion region gets **wider**.

## Side by side

| | Forward (+ on p) | Reverse (+ on n) |
| Barrier | lower, $e(V_0 - V)$ | higher, $e(V_0 + \vert V\vert)$ |
| Who crosses | majority carriers | minority carriers |
| How | diffusion | drift |
| Current | large, grows fast with $V$ | tiny, constant |
| Depletion region | thinner | wider |

Under bias $E_F$ is not flat any more. The n-side and p-side Fermi levels are $eV$ apart.

? In reverse bias, which carriers carry the current: majority or minority?

= Minority carriers. Electrons from the p-side and holes from the n-side, swept across by the field.

@s Why that is small

Few carriers available → tiny current. And it does not grow with voltage, because the supply of carriers is the limit.

@@topic 12 | Minority carrier injection | Tutorial Ch2 Q9, Q10
@notes
## The names

There are four numbers. The **letter** is the carrier. The **subscript** is the side it is on.

| Symbol | What it is | Value |
| $p_p$ | holes on the p-side (majority) | $N_A$ |
| $n_{p0}$ | electrons on the p-side (minority) | $n_i^2 / N_A$ |
| $n_n$ | electrons on the n-side (majority) | $N_D$ |
| $p_{n0}$ | holes on the n-side (minority) | $n_i^2 / N_D$ |

The little 0 means "with no voltage".

With no voltage, all four are flat, constant lines.

## Forward bias injects minority carriers

In forward bias, electrons pour from the n-side into the p-side (Topic 11).

Once they are in the p-side, they are **minority carriers** there. So right next to the junction, the p-side now has far more electrons than normal.

How many? This is on the formula sheet as $c = c_0\,e^{eV/kT}$:

$$n'_p = n_{p0}\,e^{V/0.026} \qquad\qquad p'_n = p_{n0}\,e^{V/0.026}$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
| $n'_p$ | electrons on the p-side, right at the edge of the depletion region |
| $p'_n$ | holes on the n-side, right at the edge of the depletion region |
| $V$ | applied voltage. + for forward, − for reverse. |

> **In words.** Take the normal minority number and multiply by $e^{V/0.026}$.
>
> That multiplier is enormous. For $V = 0.5$ V it is $2.25\times10^{8}$.

The **majority** carriers hardly change. They are so many already that the extra makes no difference.

? p-side doping $N_A = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³. What is $n_{p0}$?

= $n_{p0} = n_i^2/N_A = 2.1\times10^{20} \div 10^{16} = 2.1\times10^{4}$ cm⁻³.

## The shape: a straight line

At the junction edge the minority concentration is the big injected value.

At the metal **contact** at the far end, any extra carriers are removed at once. So there it is back to the normal value.

In a **short diode**, the line between those two points is **straight**.

{{fig:profile_fwd}}

("Short" means the region is so short that the carriers reach the contact before they have time to recombine.)

In this course, $L_n$ is the length of the **p-region** (where electrons are the minority), and $L_p$ is the length of the **n-region**.

## Reverse bias

Put a negative $V$ in the formula. $e^{-1/0.026}$ is about $10^{-17}$. So the minority concentration at the junction edge is **about zero**.

The field has swept them away.

At the contact it is still the normal value. Straight line between.

{{fig:profile_rev}}

## Stored charge

The extra carriers in forward bias are the shaded **triangle**.

Area of a triangle = ½ × base × height. Multiply by $e$ to turn carriers into charge:

$$\frac{Q}{A} = e\times\tfrac12\times(\text{height})\times(\text{length})$$

| | Height | Length |
| Extra electrons in the p-region | $n'_p$ | $L_n$ |
| Extra holes in the n-region | $p'_n$ | $L_p$ |

(Strictly the height is $n'_p - n_{p0}$, but $n_{p0}$ is far too small to matter.)

The answer is in C/cm².

@s The idea
The extra carriers make a triangle (see Q9b). Charge per area = $e$ × area of the triangle.

$$\frac{Q}{A} = e\times\tfrac12\times\text{height}\times\text{length}$$
@s Holes in the n-region
Height $p'_n = 4.7\times10^{10}$. Length $L_p = 8\times10^{-3}$ cm.

$$\frac{Q_p}{A} = 1.6\times10^{-19}\times\tfrac12\times4.7\times10^{10}\times8\times10^{-3} = 3.0\times10^{-11}\ \text{C/cm}^2$$
@s Electrons in the p-region
Height $n'_p = 4.7\times10^{13}$. Length $L_n = 5\times10^{-3}$ cm.

$$\frac{Q_n}{A} = 1.6\times10^{-19}\times\tfrac12\times4.7\times10^{13}\times5\times10^{-3} = 1.9\times10^{-8}\ \text{C/cm}^2$$

@@topic 13 | Diode current | Tutorial Ch2 Q12, Q13
@notes
## The idea

In Topic 12 the injected minority carriers made a sloping straight line.

In Topic 4, a slope in concentration means a **diffusion current**.

Put those together and you have the diode current. Nothing new.

## Current from the slope

Diffusion current = $e \times D \times$ slope.

The line is straight, so slope = height ÷ length:

$$J_n = e\,D_n\,\frac{n'_p}{L_n} \qquad\qquad J_p = e\,D_p\,\frac{p'_n}{L_p}$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
| $J_n$ | current carried by **electrons**, diffusing through the **p-region** |
| $J_p$ | current carried by **holes**, diffusing through the **n-region** |
| $D_n$, $D_p$ | diffusion coefficients (cm²/s) |
| $n'_p$, $p'_n$ | injected minority concentrations at the junction (Topic 12) |
| $L_n$, $L_p$ | length of the p-region, length of the n-region |

Total: $J = J_n + J_p$. To get amps, multiply by the area of the diode.

## The formula sheet version

The sheet writes the same thing with $n'_p = n_{p0}\,e^{eV/kT}$ put in, and a $-1$:

$$J_n = \frac{e\,D_n\,n_{p0}}{L_n}\left(e^{eV/kT} - 1\right) \qquad\qquad J_p = \frac{e\,D_p\,p_{n0}}{L_p}\left(e^{eV/kT} - 1\right)$$

The $-1$ is there because the height of the triangle is really $n'_p - n_{p0}$.

- **Forward bias:** the exponential is huge. Ignore the $-1$.
- **Reverse bias:** the exponential is about 0. The bracket is $-1$. A small, constant, negative current.

{{fig:iv}}

## Which carrier carries most of the current?

Look at what sets the size: $n_{p0} = n_i^2/N_A$ and $p_{n0} = n_i^2/N_D$.

A **lightly** doped side has **more** minority carriers.

> So most of the current is carried by the carriers injected **into the lightly doped side**.

Example: $N_A$ small, $N_D$ big. Then $n_{p0}$ is big, so $J_n$ wins. The current is nearly all electrons.

? A diode has $N_A = 10^{19}$ and $N_D = 10^{15}$ cm⁻³. Is the current mostly electrons or mostly holes?

= Mostly holes. The n-side is the lightly doped one, so $p_{n0} = n_i^2/N_D$ is the big one, and $J_p$ wins.

## Changing the length

$J$ has the length on the bottom.

Halve a region → the same drop happens over half the distance → the slope doubles → **that region's current doubles**.

The other region's current does not change.

@q Ch2 Q12
The Q9 diode: $N_A = 10^{15}$, $N_D = 10^{18}$ cm⁻³. p-region length $L_n = 5\times10^{-3}$ cm, n-region length $L_p = 8\times10^{-3}$ cm. Bias 0.5 V forward. $D_n$ = 35 cm²/s, $D_p$ = 12.5 cm²/s. Find the hole and electron current densities. Why is the electron current larger? Give the total.
@s Injected carriers (from Q9b)
$$n'_p = 4.7\times10^{13}\ \text{cm}^{-3} \qquad p'_n = 4.7\times10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$$
@s Electron current
Electrons diffuse through the p-region, length $L_n$:

$$J_n = \frac{e\,D_n\,n'_p}{L_n} = \frac{1.6\times10^{-19}\times35\times4.7\times10^{13}}{5\times10^{-3}} = 5.3\times10^{-2}\ \text{A/cm}^2$$
@s Hole current
Holes diffuse through the n-region, length $L_p$:

$$J_p = \frac{e\,D_p\,p'_n}{L_p} = \frac{1.6\times10^{-19}\times12.5\times4.7\times10^{10}}{8\times10^{-3}} = 1.2\times10^{-5}\ \text{A/cm}^2$$
@s Why is J_n bigger?
The p-side is the **lightly doped** side ($10^{15}$ against $10^{18}$).

So it has many more minority carriers: $n_{p0}$ is 1000 times bigger than $p_{n0}$. That makes $n'_p$ 1000 times bigger than $p'_n$.
@s Total
$$J = J_n + J_p = 5.3\times10^{-2} + 0.0012\times10^{-2} \approx 5.3\times10^{-2}\ \text{A/cm}^2$$
@a $J_n = 5.3\times10^{-2}$ A/cm², $J_p = 1.2\times10^{-5}$ A/cm². The electron current is larger because the p-side is lightly doped, so far more electrons are injected into it. Total ≈ $5.3\times10^{-2}$ A/cm².

@q Ch2 Q13
The n-region in Q12 is halved in length. What happens to the total current?
@s Which current uses the n-region?
The **hole** current. Holes are the minority carrier diffusing through the n-region.
@s Halve the length
$L_p$ is on the bottom of $J_p$. Half the length → double the slope → double the current:

$$J_p = 2\times1.2\times10^{-5} = 2.4\times10^{-5}\ \text{A/cm}^2$$
@s Electron current
The p-region has not changed. $J_n$ stays at $5.3\times10^{-2}$ A/cm².
@s Total
$J_p$ was only a tiny part of the total to begin with. Doubling it changes almost nothing.
@a The hole current doubles to $2.4\times10^{-5}$ A/cm², but the total stays at about $5.3\times10^{-2}$ A/cm², because the electron current dominates.


@@topic 14 | Depletion width, charge and electric field | Tutorial Ch2 Q11 b to e
@notes
## Where we are

Topic 10 said: near the junction there is a strip with **no carriers**, only fixed charged atoms.

This topic puts numbers on that strip:

1. how much charge is in it
2. how wide it is
3. how strong the field is

Set up the axis like this: the junction is at $x = 0$. The strip reaches $w_p$ into the p-side and $w_n$ into the n-side.

## Step 1: the charge sitting there

**p-side of the strip.** The holes are gone. What is left is acceptor atoms. Each one has charge $-e$. There are $N_A$ of them per cm³.

$$\text{charge density} = -e\,N_A$$

**n-side of the strip.** The electrons are gone. What is left is donor atoms. Each one has charge $+e$. There are $N_D$ per cm³.

$$\text{charge density} = +e\,N_D$$

**Outside the strip.** Carriers cancel the dopant atoms. Charge density = 0.

Plot that against $x$ and you get two **blocks**. Look at the top half of the diagram below.

## Step 2: the two blocks must balance

The diode as a whole has no net charge.

So the negative charge on the p-side must equal the positive charge on the n-side.

Charge in a block = density × width. So:

$$N_A\,w_p = N_D\,w_n$$

> **What it means.** If one side is heavily doped, it only needs a **thin** slice to supply the charge. The lightly doped side needs a **wide** slice to match it.
>
> **So the depletion region is mostly in the lightly doped side.**
>
> **Use it when** you know one width and want the other, or you are asked why one side is wider.
>
> Not on the formula sheet. Learn it.

{{fig:rhoE}}

? $N_A = 10^{18}$ and $N_D = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³. The strip reaches 0.3 μm into the n-side. How far does it reach into the p-side?

= $w_p = w_n \times N_D/N_A = 0.3 \times 10^{16}/10^{18} = 0.003$ μm. One hundred times thinner, because the p-side is one hundred times more heavily doped.

## Step 3: how wide

From the formula sheet:

$$w_n = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\varepsilon\,(V_0 - V)}{e}\cdot\frac{N_A}{N_D\,(N_A + N_D)}}$$

$$w_p = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\varepsilon\,(V_0 - V)}{e}\cdot\frac{N_D}{N_A\,(N_A + N_D)}}$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Value |
| $\varepsilon$ | permittivity of Si $= \varepsilon_0 \times \varepsilon_r$ | $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-14}$ F/cm. $\varepsilon_r$ = 11.9 in the tutorial answers. |
| $V_0$ | built-in voltage (Topic 10) | V |
| $V$ | applied voltage. + forward, − reverse. No bias: $V = 0$. | V |
| $e$ | | $1.6\times10^{-19}$ C |

> **Units trap.** The sheet gives $\varepsilon_0$ in F/**m**. Doping is in cm⁻³. Use $8.85\times10^{-14}$ F/**cm** so the width comes out in cm.

**What the voltage does:**

- Forward bias: $V_0 - V$ gets smaller → strip gets **thinner**.
- Reverse bias: $V_0 - V = V_0 + |V|$ gets bigger → strip gets **wider**.

**The shortcut.** If one side is much more heavily doped, say $N_A$ far bigger than $N_D$, then $\dfrac{N_A}{N_A + N_D} \approx 1$ and:

$$w_n \approx \sqrt{\frac{2\,\varepsilon\,(V_0 - V)}{e\,N_D}}$$

Then get $w_p$ from the balance in Step 2. This is what the official Q11 answer does.

## Step 4: the electric field

Field lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge. So the field runs from the n-side blocks to the p-side blocks: it points in the $-x$ direction. That is why its value is **negative**.

How does it change with $x$? Poisson's equation (formula sheet):

$$\frac{d\mathcal{E}}{dx} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon}$$

> **In words.** The **slope** of the field = the charge density ÷ $\varepsilon$.

Now walk across the diode from left to right. Follow the bottom half of the diagram.

1. **Before the strip.** No charge. Slope is zero. The field is 0.
2. **p-side of the strip.** Charge is negative. So the field slopes **down**, in a straight line.
3. **At the junction, $x = 0$.** The field is at its most negative. Call it $\mathcal{E}_0$.
4. **n-side of the strip.** Charge is positive. So the field slopes back **up**, in a straight line.
5. **End of the strip.** Back to 0.

The result is a **triangle**. Its tip is at the junction.

To get the size of the tip: on the n-side the field climbs at a rate $eN_D/\varepsilon$ over a distance $w_n$, and ends at 0. So it must have started at:

$$\mathcal{E}_0 = -\frac{e\,N_D\,w_n}{\varepsilon}$$

> **Use it when** you are asked for the maximum field, the field at the junction, or the "minimum" field. ("Minimum" because it is the most negative value.)

? Where in the diode is the field strongest?

= Right at the junction, $x = 0$. It is zero at both edges of the depletion region.

## Step 5: the total charge

Charge = density × volume. The volume of the n-side block is $w_n \times A$:

$$Q = e\,N_D\,w_n\,A$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
| $Q$ | charge on the n-side (positive). The p-side has $-Q$. |
| $A$ | area of the diode. For a round diode of diameter $d$: $A = \pi\,(d/2)^2$, in cm². |

## The order to do a question in

1. $V_0$ (Topic 10)
2. $w_n$ and $w_p$ (Step 3, then Step 2)
3. $Q$ (Step 5)
4. $\mathcal{E}_0$ (Step 4)

@q Ch2 Q11 b–e
pn diode, diameter 100 μm. $N_A = 10^{19}$, $N_D = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³. $T$ = 300 K. From part (a), $V_0$ = 0.88 V. Find:

b. the depletion width in the n and p material
c. a reason why the n-side width is bigger
d. the charge $Q$ on each side
e. the minimum electric field
@s (b) Work out ε
$$\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\,\varepsilon_r = 8.85\times10^{-14}\times11.9 = 1.05\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/cm}$$
@s (b) Width on the n-side
$N_A$ is 1000 times $N_D$, so use the shortcut. No bias, so $V = 0$.

$$w_n = \sqrt{\frac{2\,\varepsilon\,V_0}{e\,N_D}} = \sqrt{\frac{2\times1.05\times10^{-12}\times0.88}{1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{16}}}$$

$$w_n = \sqrt{1.16\times10^{-9}} = 3.4\times10^{-5}\ \text{cm}\quad(0.34\ \mu\text{m})$$
@s (b) Width on the p-side
Use the balance $N_A\,w_p = N_D\,w_n$:

$$w_p = w_n\times\frac{N_D}{N_A} = 3.4\times10^{-5}\times\frac{10^{16}}{10^{19}} = 3.4\times10^{-8}\ \text{cm}$$
@s (c) Why is the n-side wider?
Both sides must hold the same amount of charge: $N_A\,w_p = N_D\,w_n$.

The n-side has 1000 times fewer dopant atoms per cm³. So it needs a strip 1000 times wider to match.
@s (d) Area of the diode
Radius = 50 μm = $50\times10^{-4}$ cm.

$$A = \pi\,(50\times10^{-4})^2 = 7.85\times10^{-5}\ \text{cm}^2$$
@s (d) Charge
$$Q = e\,N_D\,w_n\,A = 1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{16}\times3.4\times10^{-5}\times7.85\times10^{-5}$$

$$Q = 4.3\times10^{-12}\ \text{C}$$

n-side: $+4.3\times10^{-12}$ C. p-side: $-4.3\times10^{-12}$ C.
@s (e) Field at the junction
$$\mathcal{E}_0 = -\frac{e\,N_D\,w_n}{\varepsilon} = -\frac{1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{16}\times3.4\times10^{-5}}{1.05\times10^{-12}}$$

$$\mathcal{E}_0 = -5.2\times10^{4}\ \text{V/cm}$$
