@@topic 1 | The bond model: electrons and holes | Tutorial Ch1 Q1, Q2, Q3
@notes
## Start here: what silicon looks like

A silicon atom has **4 outer electrons**.

In a crystal, each Si atom sits next to 4 other Si atoms.
It shares one electron with each neighbour.

Two shared electrons between two atoms = **one bond**.

So every atom has 4 bonds, and every bond holds 2 electrons.

## At 0 K nothing can move

At absolute zero, every electron is locked in a bond.

No loose charges means no current.
Silicon at 0 K is an **insulator**.

## At 300 K heat knocks a few electrons loose

Heat makes the atoms shake.
Now and then, the shaking knocks an electron out of its bond.

That one event makes **two** things:

- a **free electron**. Negative. It can wander through the crystal.
- a **hole**. The empty place left in the bond.

{{fig:lattice_T}}

? One bond breaks. How many free electrons and how many holes did that make?

= One of each. They are always made in pairs.

## Why a hole counts as a moving positive charge

A hole is an empty seat in a bond.

An electron from the bond next door can hop into it.
Now the empty seat is next door.

So the empty seat **moves**, in the opposite direction to the electron that hopped.

A missing negative charge that moves around behaves just like a **positive charge that moves around**.
That is why we treat a hole as a positive carrier.

A hole is **not** a missing atom, and it is **not** a proton.

## The names you need

- $n$ = number of free electrons in each cm³
- $p$ = number of holes in each cm³
- $n_i$ = the value of both in pure silicon

Pure silicon is called **intrinsic**. Every bond that breaks gives one of each, so:

$$n = p = n_i = 1.45\times10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}\quad\text{(Si at 300 K)}$$

That looks big, but 1 cm³ of Si has about $5\times10^{22}$ atoms. So almost every bond is still whole.

Hotter means more broken bonds, so $n_i$ goes up with temperature.

## The law of mass action

$$n \times p = n_i^{2}$$

> **What it says.** Multiply the electrons by the holes and you always get the same number, $n_i^2 = 2.1\times10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-6}$.
>
> **Why.** If you add extra electrons, they fill up holes. So more electrons means fewer holes.
>
> **Use it when** you know one of $n$ or $p$ and need the other.
>
> It is **not** on the formula sheet. Learn it.

## Doping: adding carriers on purpose

Pure Si has very few carriers. We add other atoms to get more.

**Phosphorus (P)** has **5** outer electrons.
4 of them make the bonds. 1 is spare.
The spare one is a **free electron**. No hole is made.
Silicon with P is **n-type** (n for negative carriers).

**Boron (B)** has only **3** outer electrons.
One of its 4 bonds is missing an electron.
That gap is a **hole**. No free electron is made.
Silicon with B is **p-type** (p for positive carriers).

{{fig:lattice_dope}}

? Arsenic has 5 outer electrons. Does it make Si n-type or p-type?

= n-type. Same as phosphorus: 4 electrons bond, 1 is spare.

@@topic 2 | Doping: how many electrons and holes | Tutorial Ch1 Q4, Q5
@notes
## The one question this topic answers

"I know the doping. What are $n$ and $p$?"

You will do this at the start of almost every later question. So get it solid.

## Rule 1: mass action (from Topic 1)

$$n \times p = n_i^{2}$$

## Rule 2: charge neutrality

A lump of silicon has no overall charge.
So all the positive charges must equal all the negative charges.

$$p + N_D = n + N_A$$

| Symbol | What it is | Charge |
| $p$ | holes | + |
| $N_D$ | donor atoms (like P). Each gave away an electron, so it is left positive. | + |
| $n$ | free electrons | − |
| $N_A$ | acceptor atoms (like B). Each took an electron, so it is left negative. | − |

The tutorial writes it as $-n + p + N_D^{+} - N_A^{-} = 0$. That is the same equation, moved to one side.

It is **not** on the formula sheet. Learn it.

## The shortcut you use nearly every time

Doping is usually $10^{15}$ to $10^{19}$ cm⁻³.
That is hugely bigger than $n_i = 10^{10}$.

So the dopant atoms decide everything:

> **n-type** (donors, $N_D$):
>
> $$n = N_D \qquad\qquad p = \frac{n_i^{2}}{N_D}$$
>
> **p-type** (acceptors, $N_A$):
>
> $$p = N_A \qquad\qquad n = \frac{n_i^{2}}{N_A}$$

In words:

- **Majority** carrier = the doping.
- **Minority** carrier = $n_i^2$ ÷ the doping.

Use $n_i^{2} = 2.1\times10^{20}\ \text{cm}^{-6}$.

**Example.** n-type, $N_D = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³.

$$n = 10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3} \qquad p = \frac{2.1\times10^{20}}{10^{16}} = 2.1\times10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$$

? p-type Si with $N_A = 10^{17}$ cm⁻³. What are $p$ and $n$?

= $p = N_A = 10^{17}$ cm⁻³. Then $n = 2.1\times10^{20} \div 10^{17} = 2100$ cm⁻³.

## Units trap

Sometimes the doping is given in **m⁻³**. Everything else is in **cm⁻³**.

1 m³ = $10^{6}$ cm³, so:

$$\text{value in cm}^{-3} = \frac{\text{value in m}^{-3}}{10^{6}}$$

? Convert $10^{22}$ m⁻³ to cm⁻³.

= $10^{22} \div 10^{6} = 10^{16}$ cm⁻³.

## Where the shortcut comes from

Tutorial Q4 asks you to derive it. The steps are in that question. The idea is just:

put Rule 1 into Rule 2, solve, then notice the doping is far bigger than $n_i$.

@q Ch1 Q4
(1) Write the law of mass action. (2) Write charge neutrality. (3) Derive the electron and hole concentrations in p-type Si.
@s Mass action
$$n \times p = n_i^{2}$$
@s Charge neutrality
Positives = negatives:

$$p + N_D = n + N_A$$
@s Make it p-type
p-type has only acceptors, so $N_D = 0$:

$$p = n + N_A$$
@s Get rid of n
From mass action, $n = \dfrac{n_i^{2}}{p}$. Put that in:

$$p = \frac{n_i^{2}}{p} + N_A$$
@s Solve for p
Multiply through by $p$:

$$p^{2} - N_A\,p - n_i^{2} = 0$$

This is a quadratic in $p$. Take the positive root:

$$p = \frac{N_A + \sqrt{N_A^{2} + 4n_i^{2}}}{2}$$
@s Simplify
$N_A$ is far bigger than $n_i$, so the $4n_i^2$ under the root does nothing. The root is just $N_A$:

$$p \approx \frac{N_A + N_A}{2} = N_A$$

Then mass action gives the electrons:

$$n = \frac{n_i^{2}}{N_A}$$
@a In p-type Si, $p \approx N_A$ and $n = n_i^{2}/N_A$.

@@topic 3 | Drift: mobility, conductivity, resistance | Tutorial Ch1 Q6, Q7
@notes
## The idea

Carriers are always jiggling about in random directions. On average they go nowhere.

Put a voltage across the silicon. Now there is an electric field $\mathcal{E}$ inside it.

The field gives every carrier a steady push. They still jiggle, but now they slowly **drift** one way.

That drift is a current.

{{fig:drift}}

## How fast they drift

$$v = \mu\,\mathcal{E}$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Units |
| $v$ | drift speed | cm/s |
| $\mu$ | **mobility**: how easily the carrier moves | cm²/(V s) |
| $\mathcal{E}$ | electric field | V/cm |

Bigger field, faster drift. Bigger mobility, faster drift.

## What decides the mobility

A carrier speeds up in the field until it **hits something**. Then it starts again.

$$\mu = \frac{e\,\tau}{m}$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
| $\tau$ | average time between collisions |
| $m$ | mass of the carrier |

A long time between collisions means more time to speed up. So big $\tau$ gives big $\mu$.

What do carriers hit?

- the shaking Si atoms
- **dopant atoms**

More doping means more things to hit. So $\tau$ gets shorter and **mobility goes down**.

{{img:03/mobility-graph.png|Mobility against doping. Find the doping along the bottom. Go up to the red line (electrons) or the blue line (holes). Read the value on the left.}}

Notice: electrons (red) are always more mobile than holes (blue).

? The doping goes from $10^{16}$ to $10^{18}$ cm⁻³. What happens to the electron mobility?

= It falls, from about 1200 to about 250 cm²/(V s). More dopant atoms to bump into.

## Conductivity

How well does the material conduct?

$$\sigma = e\,(n\,\mu_n + p\,\mu_p)$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Units |
| $\sigma$ | conductivity | (Ω cm)⁻¹ |
| $e$ | charge of one carrier, $1.6\times10^{-19}$ | C |
| $n$, $p$ | how many electrons, holes | cm⁻³ |
| $\mu_n$, $\mu_p$ | how easily each one moves | cm²/(V s) |

> **In words.** Charge × how many × how easily they move. Do it for electrons, do it for holes, add.
>
> **Shortcut.** In doped Si the minority carriers are so few you can drop them:
>
> n-type: $\sigma \approx e\,n\,\mu_n$. p-type: $\sigma \approx e\,p\,\mu_p$.
>
> In intrinsic Si, $n = p$, so **keep both** terms.

Resistivity is just the flip: $\rho = 1/\sigma$.

## Resistance of a bar

$$R = \frac{L}{\sigma\,A}$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Units |
| $L$ | length, **along** the current | cm |
| $A$ | area the current passes **through** | cm² |

Longer bar, more resistance. Fatter bar, less resistance.

**Convert everything to cm first.**

$$1\ \mu\text{m} = 10^{-4}\ \text{cm} \qquad 1\ \text{nm} = 10^{-7}\ \text{cm} \qquad 1\ \text{m} = 100\ \text{cm}$$

? You make the bar twice as long. What happens to $R$?

= It doubles. $L$ is on top of the fraction.

@q Ch1 Q6
The mobility of electrons and holes in Si depends on the doping concentration. Explain why.
@s What mobility depends on
$$\mu = \frac{e\,\tau}{m}$$

$\tau$ is the average time between collisions.
@s What doping does
Dopant atoms are different from the Si atoms around them. They break up the regular pattern of the lattice.

Carriers scatter off them.
@s Put it together
More doping → more collisions → shorter $\tau$ → smaller $\mu$.
@a Dopant atoms scatter the carriers. More doping means a shorter time between collisions, so the mobility falls.

@@topic 4 | Diffusion and the built-in field | Tutorial Ch1 Q8, Q9
@notes
## The idea

Put a drop of ink in water. It spreads out until it is even.

Carriers do the same. They move from where there are **many** to where there are **few**.

This is **diffusion**. It needs no voltage. It only needs an uneven concentration.

{{fig:diffusion}}

## Diffusion current

$$J_n = e\,D_n\,\frac{dn}{dx} \qquad\qquad J_p = -\,e\,D_p\,\frac{dp}{dx}$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Units |
| $J$ | current density (current per area) | A/cm² |
| $D$ | diffusion coefficient: how fast the carrier spreads | cm²/s |
| $\dfrac{dn}{dx}$ | the **slope** of the concentration | cm⁻⁴ |

> **In words.** Steeper slope, more current. No slope, no diffusion current.
>
> **Why the signs differ.** Both carriers move downhill. But electrons are negative, so their current points the other way from their motion.
>
> **Straight line?** Then the slope is just (change in $n$) ÷ (distance).

? The electron concentration is the same everywhere. What is the diffusion current?

= Zero. The slope is zero.

## Total current

A carrier can drift (Topic 3) and diffuse at once. Add them. This is on the formula sheet:

$$J_n = e\,\mu_n\,n\,\mathcal{E} + e\,D_n\,\frac{dn}{dx}$$

$$J_p = e\,\mu_p\,p\,\mathcal{E} - e\,D_p\,\frac{dp}{dx}$$

First part = drift. Second part = diffusion.

## The Einstein relation

$D$ and $\mu$ both say how easily a carrier moves. So they are linked:

$$D = \frac{kT}{e}\,\mu \qquad\qquad \frac{kT}{e} = 0.026\ \text{V at 300 K}$$

**Use it when** you need to swap $D/\mu$ for a number.

## Uneven doping makes its own electric field

Take an n-type bar. Dope it **heavily on the left** and **lightly on the right**.

Follow what happens:

1. There are more electrons on the left. So electrons **diffuse to the right**.
2. They leave their donor atoms behind. Those are fixed and **positive**. So the left becomes a bit positive.
3. The right has extra electrons. So the right becomes a bit **negative**.
4. Positive on the left, negative on the right: that is an **electric field pointing right**.
5. That field pulls electrons back to the left (drift).
6. It settles when drift exactly cancels diffusion. Then **no current flows**: $J_n = 0$.

> **Rule.** The built-in field points **towards the lightly doped end**.

## Getting the field

Set the total electron current to zero:

$$e\,\mu_n\,n\,\mathcal{E} + e\,D_n\,\frac{dn}{dx} = 0$$

Rearrange for $\mathcal{E}$:

$$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{D_n}{\mu_n}\,\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{dn}{dx}$$

Swap $D_n/\mu_n$ for $kT/e$ (Einstein):

$$\mathcal{E} = -\frac{kT}{e}\,\frac{1}{n}\,\frac{dn}{dx}$$

**Use it when** the doping changes along the sample and there is no voltage. Put $n = N(x)$, the doping.

? The doping falls as you go right. Is $dn/dx$ positive or negative? Which way does $\mathcal{E}$ point?

= $dn/dx$ is negative. The minus sign in front makes $\mathcal{E}$ positive, so it points right, towards the light doping.

@q Ch1 Q8
In an n-Si layer at 300 K, the electron concentration falls in a straight line from $10^{18}$ cm⁻³ to $7\times10^{17}$ cm⁻³ over 0.10 cm. $D_n$ = 50 cm²/s. Find the diffusion current density.
@s Find the slope
It is a straight line, so slope = change ÷ distance.

$$\text{change} = 10^{18} - 7\times10^{17} = 3\times10^{17}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$$

$$\text{slope} = \frac{3\times10^{17}}{0.10} = 3\times10^{18}\ \text{cm}^{-4}$$
@s Use the electron diffusion equation
$$J_n = e\,D_n\,\frac{dn}{dx}$$
@s Put the numbers in
$$J_n = 1.6\times10^{-19}\times50\times3\times10^{18} = 24\ \text{A/cm}^2$$
@s Direction
Electrons move towards the low end. Current is opposite to electron motion, so it points back towards the high end.
@a $J_n = 24$ A/cm², pointing towards the high-concentration end.

@q Ch1 Q9
n-type Si is 0.04 cm long. Its doping changes along $x$:

$$N(x) = 10^{17} - 2.5\times10^{18}\,x \qquad (N\text{ in cm}^{-3},\ x\text{ in cm})$$

@s Electrons follow the doping
It is n-type, so $n(x) = N(x)$.

That is a straight line. It starts at $10^{17}$ at $x = 0$ and falls to zero at $x = 0.04$ cm.
@s Holes from mass action
$$p(x) = \frac{n_i^{2}}{N(x)}$$

{{img:04/ans-q9-sketch.png|Official sketch. Blue straight line = electrons (right-hand axis). Red curve = holes (left-hand axis).}}

@@topic 5 | Energy bands and the Fermi level | Tutorial Ch1 Q10 to Q15
@notes
## Why we switch pictures

The bond picture is good for seeing what a hole is.
But it cannot give you numbers.

The **band picture** can. Instead of drawing where electrons are, we draw their **energy**.

## Two bands and a gap

Electrons in silicon can only have certain energies.

- **Valence band.** Electrons stuck in bonds. Its top edge is $E_V$.
- **Conduction band.** Free electrons. Its bottom edge is $E_C$.
- **The gap.** Between them there are no allowed energies at all.

$$E_G = E_C - E_V = 1.12\ \text{eV for Si}$$

**Link to Topic 1.** Breaking a bond = lifting an electron from the valence band up to the conduction band. It costs $E_G$. It leaves a hole behind in the valence band.

So: free electrons live just above $E_C$. Holes live just below $E_V$.

## The Fermi function: how full is a state?

$$f(E) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(E - E_F)/kT}}$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
| $f(E)$ | the chance that a state at energy $E$ has an electron in it (0 to 1) |
| $E_F$ | the **Fermi level** |
| $kT$ | thermal energy. 0.026 eV at 300 K. $k = 8.62\times10^{-5}$ eV/K |

{{fig:fermi}}

Three things to read off it:

- Far **below** $E_F$: $f \approx 1$. Full.
- Exactly **at** $E_F$: $f = \tfrac12$. Always. Put $E = E_F$ in and you get $1/(1+e^{0}) = 1/2$.
- Far **above** $E_F$: $f \approx 0$. Empty.

The chance a state is **empty** is $1 - f$.

? In heavily doped n-type Si, what is the chance of finding an electron in a state exactly at $E_F$?

= ½. It is always ½ at $E_F$. Doping moves $E_F$, but it does not change this.

## E_F shows you the doping

Think of $E_F$ as a water level.

- $E_F$ high, near $E_C$: the conduction band gets lots of electrons. **n-type.**
- $E_F$ low, near $E_V$: the valence band gets lots of holes. **p-type.**
- $E_F$ in the middle: few of each. **Intrinsic.** This middle position is called $E_i$.

{{fig:bands3}}

## From E_F to numbers

On the formula sheet:

$$n = N_C\,e^{-(E_C - E_F)/kT} \qquad\qquad p = N_V\,e^{-(E_F - E_V)/kT}$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Value for Si |
| $N_C$ | number of states at the bottom of the conduction band | $3.2\times10^{19}$ cm⁻³ |
| $N_V$ | number of states at the top of the valence band | $1.8\times10^{19}$ cm⁻³ |
| $E_C - E_F$ | how far $E_F$ is below the conduction band | eV |
| $E_F - E_V$ | how far $E_F$ is above the valence band | eV |

> **In words.** The further $E_F$ is from a band, the fewer carriers in that band.
>
> **Going backwards.** If you know the doping and want where $E_F$ sits, take logs:
>
> $$E_C - E_F = kT\,\ln\!\frac{N_C}{n} \qquad\qquad E_F - E_V = kT\,\ln\!\frac{N_V}{p}$$

**Example.** n-type, $N_D = 10^{17}$ cm⁻³. So $n = 10^{17}$.

$$E_C - E_F = 0.026\,\ln\!\frac{3.2\times10^{19}}{10^{17}} = 0.026\times5.77 = 0.15\ \text{eV}$$

$E_F$ sits 0.15 eV below $E_C$.

? p-type, $N_A = 1.8\times10^{17}$ cm⁻³. How far is $E_F$ above $E_V$?

= $p = 1.8\times10^{17}$. So $E_F - E_V = 0.026\,\ln(1.8\times10^{19} \div 1.8\times10^{17}) = 0.026\,\ln(100) = 0.12$ eV.

## The same thing, measured from the middle

Sometimes it is easier to measure from $E_i$ instead of from the band edges:

$$n = n_i\,e^{(E_F - E_i)/kT} \qquad\qquad p = n_i\,e^{(E_i - E_F)/kT}$$

Check it makes sense: if $E_F = E_i$ then $n = p = n_i$. Good, that is intrinsic.

Going backwards: $E_F - E_i = kT\,\ln(n/n_i)$.

Tutorial Q13 derives these. Q15 finds where $E_i$ is.

@q Ch1 Q10
A material has its Fermi level at 6.25 eV. At what temperature is there a 1% chance that a state 0.3 eV **below** the Fermi level is **empty**?
@s Turn the words into f
1% chance of empty means 99% chance of full:

$$f = 0.99$$

The state is 0.3 eV below $E_F$, so $E - E_F = -0.3$ eV.

(The 6.25 eV is not needed. Only the difference matters.)
@s Put it in the Fermi function
$$0.99 = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-0.3/kT}}$$
@s Get the exponential on its own
Flip both sides:

$$1 + e^{-0.3/kT} = \frac{1}{0.99} = 1.0101$$

$$e^{-0.3/kT} = 0.0101$$
@s Take logs
$$-\frac{0.3}{kT} = \ln(0.0101) = -4.595$$

$$kT = \frac{0.3}{4.595} = 0.0653\ \text{eV}$$
@s Get T
$$T = \frac{0.0653}{8.62\times10^{-5}} = 757\ \text{K}$$
@a $T \approx 757$ K. (The official answer says 756 K. The difference is rounding.)

@q Ch1 Q15
Derive the position of $E_i$ in terms of the band gap.
@s Intrinsic means n = p
With $E_F = E_i$ in both formulas:

$$N_C\,e^{-(E_C - E_i)/kT} = N_V\,e^{-(E_i - E_V)/kT}$$
@s Choose where zero is
Measure energy from the valence band edge: $E_V = 0$. Then $E_C = E_G$.

$$N_C\,e^{-(E_G - E_i)/kT} = N_V\,e^{-E_i/kT}$$
@s Collect the exponentials

$$e^{(2E_i - E_G)/kT} = \frac{N_V}{N_C}$$

@notes
## Why we need a new reference line

Soon we will put two different materials side by side.
To compare them we need one energy that is the same for both.

That is the **vacuum level**, $E_{vac}$.
It is the energy of an electron that has just got **out** of the material and is sitting still outside.

Draw it as a line at the very top. Measure everything **down** from it.

## Two distances down from the vacuum level

{{fig:vac}}

$$\chi = E_{vac} - E_C \qquad\qquad \phi = E_{vac} - E_F$$

| Symbol | Name | What it is |
| $\chi$ | electron affinity | from the vacuum level down to $E_C$. **Fixed** for a material. For Si, 4.05 eV. |
| $\phi$ | workfunction | from the vacuum level down to $E_F$. **Changes with doping**, because doping moves $E_F$. |

## The link between them

Look at the diagram. To get to $E_F$ you go down $\chi$ to $E_C$, then a bit further:

$$\phi = \chi + (E_C - E_F)$$

- **n-type:** $E_F$ is near $E_C$. So $\phi$ is only a little more than 4.05 eV.
- **p-type:** $E_F$ is near $E_V$. So $\phi$ is nearly $4.05 + 1.12 = 5.17$ eV.

The middle of the gap is at $4.05 + 0.56 = 4.61$ eV.

> **Quick test.** $\phi$ less than 4.61 eV → n-type. $\phi$ more than 4.61 eV → p-type.

? A Si sample has $\phi = 4.20$ eV. Is it n-type or p-type? How far is $E_F$ below $E_C$?

= n-type, because 4.20 is less than 4.61. And $E_C - E_F = \phi - \chi = 4.20 - 4.05 = 0.15$ eV.

## The two kinds of question

**Doping → workfunction.** Find $E_C - E_F$ from the doping (Topic 5), then add $\chi$.

**Workfunction → doping.** Find where $E_F$ is from $\phi$, then use the $n$ or $p$ formula (Topic 5).

@@topic 7 | Band diagrams with a voltage applied | Tutorial Ch1 Q17, Q18
@notes
## How to read the energy axis

A band diagram plots the energy of an **electron**. Up = more energy.

Electrons are like balls. They **sink** to the lowest energy they can. So they collect at the bottom of the conduction band, at $E_C$.

Holes are the opposite. They are like **bubbles**. They float **up**. So they collect at the top of the valence band, at $E_V$.

So:

- An electron with extra energy is **higher up**, above $E_C$.
- A hole with extra energy is **further down**, below $E_V$.

## A voltage tilts the bands

Electrons are negative. They are attracted to the **+** terminal.

So the + end is where an electron has **low** energy. The − end is where it has **high** energy.

That means the bands **slope**. Low at +, high at −.

{{fig:tilt}}

Things to notice:

- $E_C$, $E_V$ and $E_F$ all tilt **together**, staying parallel. The doping is the same everywhere, so the spacing between them cannot change.
- The total height difference from one end to the other is $eV$.
- The field $\mathcal{E}$ points from + to −. The bands go **up** in the direction of $\mathcal{E}$.

## Which way do the carriers go?

- Electrons roll **downhill**, towards +.
- Holes float **uphill**, towards −.

Both give current in the same direction: from + to − through the bar.

? The + terminal is on the **right**. Which way do the bands slope, and which way do electrons move?

= The bands slope down to the right, because + is the low end. Electrons roll down to the right.

@q Ch1 Q17
On a band diagram, where do electrons with higher energy sit? Where do holes with higher energy sit?
@s Electrons
The axis is electron energy. More energy = higher up.

So energetic electrons sit **higher** in the conduction band, further above $E_C$.
@s Holes
For holes the axis is upside down. More hole energy = further **down**.

So energetic holes sit **deeper** in the valence band, further below $E_V$.

{{img:07/ans-ch1-q17.png|Official answer sketch.}}
@a High-energy electrons are higher above $E_C$. High-energy holes are further below $E_V$.
